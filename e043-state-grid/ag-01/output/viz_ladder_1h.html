Ladder · one-sided dip-buyer · long+short mirrored

What this plan does

Buys Bitcoin when it dips 8, 16 or 24 percent below its recent highest price, sells when it bounces +5%, stops if it keeps falling −10%, and after a win waits for a small 0.75% dip to buy again. It only trades while the market is calm.

How it works: Think of a person waiting at a staircase hoping for bounces. Three steps below the recent high: if price falls to step 1 it buys; if it keeps falling it buys at steps 2 and 3 too (this is called averaging down). Each buy waits for a +5% bounce to sell; if price instead falls 10% from that buy, it sells to limit the damage. When a trade wins and sells, it waits for a small dip and buys again, recycling the same money.
SettingIn plain wordsWhy it matters
When it buysWhen price falls to 8%, 16% or 24% below the highest price of the last ~12 days. Each is a separate order with its own money.Three buy steps = three chances, but also three amounts of money at risk at once.
When it sells (profit)Always +5% above each buy price.A fixed, small goal: +5% before the stop. This is the 'reward' of the trade.
When it cuts losses (stop)Always −10% below each buy price.The 'brake': if a buy keeps falling, it accepts −10% instead of an unknown bigger loss.
Buy again after a winAfter a profitable sell, it waits for a 0.75% dip and buys again with the same money.Recycles the money so one lot of capital can trade many cycles.
Only in calm marketsIt compares the average price of the last 50 candles with the average of the last 100. If they drift apart more than 0.2%, it treats it as a 'trend' and does nothing. It only trades again when they are nearly together.This strategy makes money when prices go back and forth. In a one-way trend, 'buy the dip' keeps buying a falling knife — so it refuses to trade in trends.
Why this page existsYour original idea, made wide and careful.It shows how little a careful dip-buying ladder trades (33 rounds in 4 years) and that it still loses (profit factor 0.62).

📖 Profit factor — and the better alternatives

Profit factor = money won ÷ money lost (in dollars / USDT), counting every trade of the whole test — not each single trade.

Example tradesWonLost
Trade 1: +$40$40—
Trade 2: -$10—$10
Trade 3: +$30$30—
Totals$70$10

Profit factor = 70 / 10 = 7.0 — you won 7 dollars for every 1 you lost.

Is it a good measure? Honestly — NOT alone.

  • In dollars, not % — bigger trades count more, so two strategies with the same edge look different just because of size. Your instinct is right.
  • It forgets everything about time — order of trades, how long money was stuck, compounding. Two tests with PF 2.0 can feel completely different.
  • Huge winners can hide many losses. So we do NOT drop it — we demote it and use instead, in every page:
Better toolWhat it fixes
Expectancy per trade (in $ and in % of the money used) The true edge per trade, size-free.
Log returns per trade (ln of 1 + net %) Additive and symmetric: +50% then −50% is NOT zero for log returns, it is a real loss. Plain % hides that.
Log sum (adds all log returns) The honest total growth of the whole sequence, order- and compounding-aware.

Read order: Log sum → Expectancy per trade → Max drawdown → Sharpe. Profit factor stays as a quick sanity number only.

📚 Glossary (plain words)

Return %How much the 100,000 start money grew or shrank at the end, in percent. Growth compounds here, so it already includes everything that happened in between.
Expectancy / tradeThe AVERAGE net result of one trade, in dollars and in % of the money used in that trade. The best single number about your edge: positive = each trade pays on average, negative = each trade costs on average.
Log expectancy / tradeAverage log return per trade (ln of 1 + net %). Log returns are additive and symmetric: +50% then -50% is -ln(1.5·0.5) < 0 for log, instead of looking like 0 with plain %. The statistically 'easiest to read' version.
Log sum (additive %)Adding up all per-trade log returns. Unlike plain %, this addition is mathematically honest across order and compounding — a positive sum means the whole sequence grew.
Profit factorGross dollars won ÷ gross dollars lost, for ALL trades of this test. 1.0 = breakeven. Weak alone: it is in dollars (bigger trades count more), it forgets order, time and compounding, and a few huge winners can hide many losses. Read it only NEXT TO expectancy and log returns.
Max drawdown %The worst dip from a high point to a low point of the account, in percent. It answers: "how painful was the worst moment?" A -50% dip needs +100% to recover.
SharpeAverage return per unit of risk (how wobbly the equity was). Above ~1 is decent; negative means the ride was bumpy and losing. Not a guarantee — history only.
Win rate %Share of trades that ended in profit. A low win rate is NOT automatically bad: big wins can beat many small losses. Always read it next to Expectancy.
CommissionsMoney paid to the exchange for every fill, summed (maker/taker fees). Grids trade a lot, so fees are often the difference between profit and loss.
FillsHow many times orders actually got filled (bought or sold). More fills = more fees; fewer fills = the grid may not be doing much.
Regime flipsHow many times the filter switched between 'range' and 'trend'. Each switch can cost a flatten fee, so a nervous filter bleeds money.
